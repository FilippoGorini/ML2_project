# Block splits hold up; overlap needs evidence

The literature backs the project's evaluation design and is more cautious about its windowing choice. On splitting, there is **broad consensus across statistics, ML methodology, human activity recognition (HAR) and the official scikit-learn documentation**: random or k-fold splits over time-ordered, autocorrelated data give optimistic scores. Contiguous blocks with a buffer gap, or held-out groups (subjects, recordings), are the standard fix. The main counter-evidence, Bergmeir, Hyndman & Koo (2018), holds only for purely autoregressive models on stationary series with uncorrelated residuals, and a contact classifier on 54-channel windows meets none of those conditions. Overlapping windows are common practice, since 50% is typical and 78 to 95% appears, but the key controlled study (Dehghani et al. 2019) found that **overlap's apparent benefit disappears under subject-independent evaluation**. Its gains came from leaky evaluation, not from better models. That makes the project's stride-10 vs stride-150 result (gradient boosting 0.972 → 0.958 test F1 under leak-free block evaluation) a genuine finding worth reporting, not a foregone conclusion. It still needs two caveats: it confounds overlap with a roughly 15x difference in training-set size, and it has not been tested under the unseen-recording protocol B. No source endorses non-overlapping windows with a random split. Inflation persists because neighbouring windows stay correlated even when they share no samples. The legged-robot contact papers all use contiguous or recording-level splits with stride-1, 150-sample windows and no gaps, so the project's two protocols match the field's conventions and its gaps are stricter than any published code.

## Random splits overstate accuracy on dependent data, and nobody seriously disputes it

The core claim is textbook-level. scikit-learn's cross-validation guide says KFold and ShuffleSplit "assume the samples are independent and identically distributed" and on time series "would result in unreasonable correlation between training and testing instances". It warns that shuffling time-ordered data produces "an inflated validation score" because the model is "tested on samples that are artificially similar (close in time) to training samples" ([scikit-learn User Guide §3.1](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html)). The same guide recommends group-wise CV when samples come from "different subjects, experiments, measurement devices", which is exactly the structure of 15 separate robot recordings. Arlot & Celisse's survey of cross-validation (Statistics Surveys, 2010) states that CV requires the validation set to be independent of the training set, so "when data ... are not independent, CV must be modified". It cites a proof that CV overfits under positive correlation ([Arlot & Celisse 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.4728)). Kapoor & Narayanan's leakage taxonomy (Patterns, 2023), built from 329 affected papers across 17 fields, names this failure "[L3.2] Nonindependence between train and test samples". They add the important escape clause that it is leakage "unless the scientific claim is about a distribution that has the same dependence structure" ([Kapoor & Narayanan 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07048)).

The size of the optimism depends on the problem, but it is consistently in the same direction. In time-ordered tweet classification, random CV was "significantly worse than the blocked cross-validation, and should not be used" ([Mozetič et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194317)). In spatial ecology, a random-forest biomass model that seemed to explain over half the variance had **"quasi-null" predictive power** under spatially blocked validation ([Ploton et al. 2020, Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-18321-y)). Roberts et al. (Ecography, 2017) conclude that block CV "is nearly universally more appropriate than random cross-validation" when the goal is predicting new data ([Roberts et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.02881); claim taken from the abstract).

Sensor-based classification shows the same pattern in sharper form. The same CNN scored **99.85% under 10-fold CV but 85.1% under leave-one-subject-out** ([Gholamiangonabadi et al. 2020, IEEE Access](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Deep-Neural-Networks-for-Human-Activity-Recognition-Gholamiangonabadi-Kiselov/272023fe1b116eca2c5836cc40d60abd412413c8)). Tello et al. re-ran published HAR pipelines and found drops of **5.4 to 33.6 points** when random splits were replaced by group splits, for example MILAN random forest weighted F1 falling from 95.17 to 56.64 ([Tello et al. 2023, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11950)). Hammerla & Plötz (UbiComp 2015) showed specifically that sliding windows bias k-fold CV in activity recognition, and they proposed keeping contiguous segments together ([Hammerla & Plötz 2015](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/2750858.2807551)). The most dramatic case comes from EEG. Li et al. (IEEE TPAMI 2021) showed that published high accuracies came from block-level temporal autocorrelation in the recordings, and that accuracy "drops to chance" once the design breaks that confound ([Li et al. 2021](https://engineering.purdue.edu/~qobi/papers/tpami2021.pdf)).

## Blocks with a one-window gap are a recognised design, not an ad-hoc one

The standard alternatives all share one idea. Arlot & Celisse put it as choosing training and validation indices so that "min |i − j| > h > 0, where h controls the distance from which observations i and j are independent" ([Arlot & Celisse 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.4728)). Several named methods implement it. h-block CV (Burman, Chow & Nolan 1994) and hv-block CV (Racine 2000, Journal of Econometrics) remove h observations on each side of a contiguous test block ([Zheng 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.08904); [Racine 2000](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:99:y:2000:i:1:p:39-61)). scikit-learn's `TimeSeriesSplit` has a `gap` parameter for "samples to exclude from the end of each train set before the test set" ([sklearn TimeSeriesSplit](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.TimeSeriesSplit.html)). López de Prado's purged and embargoed CV removes training samples whose information window overlaps a test sample's, then adds a further embargo for residual correlation ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purged_cross-validation); book not read directly). For series that may not be stationary, Arlot & Celisse say "the only reasonable approach in general is the hold-out" with a deterministic cut point ([Arlot & Celisse 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.4728)). Large datasets also favour a simple hold-out, following Dietterich's recommendation as summarised by Cerqueira et al. ([Cerqueira, Torgo & Mozetič 2020, Machine Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11744)).

Protocol A, [train 70% | gap | val 15% | gap | test 15%] within each recording, is a single-origin, order-preserving hold-out with hv-block-style buffers, applied in each of 15 groups. The gap size has a citable precedent. Bergmeir et al.'s "non-dependent" CV removes training rows within "a lag distance smaller than p" of any test row, where p is the input window length ([Bergmeir, Hyndman & Koo 2018](https://robjhyndman.com/papers/cv-wp.pdf)). That is exactly a one-window gap, and it is what purging requires for windows that share raw samples. **No source states "gap = one window length" as a named rule for sliding-window classification.** It is an inference from purging and from h equal to the embedding length, and it should be presented that way. Two limits deserve honest mention. A one-window gap removes shared samples, but it does not remove longer-range dependence such as gait periodicity or slow drift. The theory ties h to the autocorrelation range, not the window length. Second, Lin et al.'s contact labels come from an offline filter that uses "future and past data points around the current timestamp" ([Lin et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.15713)), so a label right at a block edge can draw on raw signal from the next block. A 150-sample gap absorbs this provided the filter's support is shorter than 150 ms, which the paper does not state.

Protocol B, five whole recordings held out, is the leave-groups-out analogue of leave-one-subject-out. The HAR literature treats the drop from within-subject to unseen-subject scores as the honest estimate of generalisation, not a model failure. Lockhart & Weiss found personal models at 98% and impersonal ones at 76% ([Lockhart & Weiss 2014](https://www.cis.fordham.edu/wisdm/Lockhart_Weiss_HASCA.pdf)). The project's 2.4 to 3.4 point gap between protocols A and B fits that pattern and is modest by HAR standards. Kapoor & Narayanan's escape clause gives the right framing: protocol A supports a claim about later segments of recordings the model has seen, and protocol B supports the stronger claim about new terrains and gaits ([Kapoor & Narayanan 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07048)).

### What is genuinely debated

The best evidence against blocking is narrow. Bergmeir, Hyndman & Koo (Computational Statistics & Data Analysis, 2018) show that "in the case of a purely autoregressive model, the use of standard K-fold CV is possible as long as the models considered have uncorrelated errors". In their 200-point simulations, h-block CV "performs considerably worse ... as they are fitted with less data". They also warn that when models underfit and errors are correlated, CV "may yield a systematic underestimation of the error" ([BHK 2018](https://robjhyndman.com/papers/cv-wp.pdf)). The project falls outside their conditions on every count. It classifies a different variable, not the target's own lags. Contact labels are piecewise-constant, so classifier errors cluster around transitions and are serially correlated. The data are about a million samples, not 200, so losing a gap costs about 0.1% of the data. Cerqueira et al. also found that Bergmeir's stationary-synthetic results "do not reflect those obtained using real-world time series". With non-stationarity present, CV variants were systematically worse than out-of-sample methods ([Cerqueira et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11744)).

Blocking has its own documented cost: mild pessimism. Sequential and out-of-sample methods "tend to underestimate" performance ([Mozetič et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194317)) and "tend to overestimate the error" ([Cerqueira et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11744)). For a reported test score, that is the safe direction. The most legitimate criticism of protocol A is the **single fixed validation block**. Cerqueira et al. warn that with one origin the estimate is prone to that period's idiosyncrasies, and they recommend repeated hold-out over several test periods ([Cerqueira et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11744)). Gholamiangonabadi et al. argue that model selection itself should use the subject-independent scheme ([Gholamiangonabadi et al. 2020](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Deep-Neural-Networks-for-Human-Activity-Recognition-Gholamiangonabadi-Kiselov/272023fe1b116eca2c5836cc40d60abd412413c8)). Two features of the project soften the concern. Each "block" is really 15 per-recording blocks pooled, which averages out some single-origin variance. And the published protocol-B papers also tune on a single fixed within-recording validation slice ([Ordonez-Apraez et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10433)). The weakness should still be named rather than hidden.

## Overlap is standard practice, but its benefit is contested

Fixed sliding windows are "the most widely employed segmentation technique in activity recognition" ([Banos et al. 2014, Sensors](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4029702/)). Overlap of 50% is most common, and 78%, 90% and over 95% all appear in published work ([Tello et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11950)). Hammerla, Halloran & Plötz's deep-HAR benchmark (IJCAI 2016) used 50% and 78% overlap, with validation and test drawn from whole held-out runs of other subjects ([Hammerla et al. 2016](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/16/Papers/220.pdf)). That is the same structure as the project: dense overlap inside training, and evaluation data separated by run. The usual rationale is more training windows and preserved temporal context at window boundaries.

The key qualifying evidence is Dehghani, Sarbishei, Glatard & Shihab (Sensors 2019). Under subject-dependent k-fold CV, overlapping windows raised F1 by **8 to 13 points** for k-NN and decision trees. Under subject-independent CV the gain disappeared. Overlap even lowered decision-tree and k-NN F1 by 1 to 4 points on one dataset, and on the other it added only 1 to 2 points, at a cost of about 9x the data and 4x the training time. Their conclusion: "The performance improvements regarding overlapping windowing reported in the literature seem to be associated with underlying limitations of subject-dependent cross validation" ([Dehghani et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6891351/)). A companion preprint puts k-fold inflation at about 10% without overlap and about 16% with it ([Dehghani, Glatard & Shihab 2019, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.02666)).

The project's result differs from Dehghani's. Every model lost accuracy at stride 150, for example gradient boosting dropping from 0.972 to 0.958, and the evaluation was leak-free: test scored on every timestep of a gapped block. That is a real departure, and there are plausible reasons for it. Dehghani studied classical classifiers on hand-crafted features with a 200 ms step. The project trains gradient boosting and neural models whose capacity rewards more examples. Dense windows also act as shift augmentation, matching the deployment condition of predicting at every millisecond. **But the comparison confounds overlap with dataset size.** Stride 150 gives about 15x fewer training windows, so the result shows that "more shifted examples help", not that "overlap per se helps". It is also measured under protocol A, the within-recording setting, which is closest to the regime where Dehghani saw the inflated gains. The research notes found no paper that isolates the benefit of within-training overlap for high-capacity models under leak-free evaluation, so this is an open question rather than settled. Repeating the stride comparison under protocol B would answer Dehghani's objection directly.

### Non-overlapping windows with a random split would not be acceptable

Removing overlap removes shared raw samples, but it does not remove dependence. Tello et al. note that for non-overlapping windows over long-running activities, "the similarity between samples drawn in a short interval will create a strong correlation between consecutive windows" ([Tello et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11950)). They report cases where inflation persisted without overlap. An eating-detection study with non-overlapping windows scored 94.4% under random 10-fold but 89% under leave-one-subject-out. LSTM results on UCI-HAR were inflated "using either overlapping and non-overlapping windows" (both reported via Tello et al.; primaries not read). The evidence that random splits mislead comes largely from data with no overlap at all: tweets, forest plots, and EEG trials ([Mozetič et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194317); [Ploton et al. 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-18321-y); [Li et al. 2021](https://engineering.purdue.edu/~qobi/papers/tpami2021.pdf)). A recent preprint formalises the point. Mechanical overlap gives a correlation range of L/S − 1 windows, which is zero at S = L, but autocorrelation in the underlying process remains ([Shi et al. 2026, arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30721)). For this dataset, adjacent 150 ms windows at 1 kHz sit in the same gait cycle and usually the same contact phase, so a randomly assigned test window would have near-twins in training. This is an inference, not a measurement. The cheap way to confirm it is to score a random split of the stride-150 windows against the block split and show the random one is higher.

Within the test set, overlap is harmless. Scoring every timestep makes test windows overlap one another, which correlates the test errors and narrows naive confidence intervals. Because no test window shares samples with a training window, this introduces no bias.

## Contact-estimation papers use contiguous splits, stride 1, and no gaps

No paper in this subfield used a random-window split, and none mentions leakage or overlap across split boundaries. The table below summarises the conventions, taken from the papers and, where noted, their public code.

| Paper | Split | Window / stride | Gap | Reported accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lin et al. 2021, CoRL ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.15713), [code](https://github.com/UMich-CURLY/deep-contact-estimator)) | Some whole sequences held out, plus contiguous per-recording 70/15/15. Code order is val, test, train | 150 samples, stride 1, labelled at last step | None | 97.82% per leg, 93.88% 16-state |
| Ordonez-Apraez et al. 2023, RSS / ECNN ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10433), [code](https://github.com/Danfoa/MorphoSymm)) | 5 randomly chosen recordings as test. The other 10 are split per recording, first 85% train, last 15% val | 150, stride 1 (code) | None | 78.8% 16-state (as reported by MI-HGNN) |
| Butterfield et al. 2024, MI-HGNN, ICRA 2025 ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11146), [code](https://github.com/lunarlab-gatech/Morphology-Informed-HGNN)) | ECNN split, verified in code | 150, stride 1, labelled at last row | None (split at window index) | 87.7% 16-state; CNN 71.8% |
| Xie et al. 2024, MS-HGNN ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01297)) | ECNN split | 150 | Not stated | 0.875 vs MI-HGNN 0.870 |
| Fischer Abati et al. 2025, Proprioceptive Image ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14612)) | ECNN test set; train and val merged | 10 | n/a | 94.5% 16-state |

Several points follow from the table. Lin's only justification for the window is "a window size of 150 is used to allow the network to infer from the time domain", with no ablation. For the higher-rate Cassie data they used 600 ([Lin et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.15713)). Later papers keep 150 explicitly "for a fair comparison" ([Butterfield et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11146)). The Proprioceptive Image paper's w = 10 result shows 150 is a convention, not a requirement ([Fischer Abati et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14612)). ECNN's stated reason for holding out recordings is to "properly evaluate the generalization capacity of the models" and "out-of-training-distribution performance" ([Ordonez-Apraez et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10433)). That is a generalisation argument, not a leakage argument. Lin's own text says "we shuffled the training data", but the code shows this means minibatch order after a contiguous split, not window-level shuffling. Lin's and MorphoSymm's code builds windows over vertically stacked recordings, so some windows straddle two recordings, and with stride 1 and no gap the first validation window shares 149 of its 150 samples with the last training window. Each such leak touches only one boundary per recording and is small, but the project's gaps close it. **Protocol A is Lin's split made stricter, and protocol B reproduces ECNN, MI-HGNN and MS-HGNN exactly.** The one real deviation is training stride 10 instead of the published stride 1, and that should be stated as an efficiency choice. The roughly 22-point gap between Lin's 93.88% (within-recording test slices) and the same CNN's 71.8% on unseen recordings ([Butterfield et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11146)) mixes implementation differences with protocol, so it is not a controlled comparison. Its direction agrees with the project's own A-vs-B gap.

## Conclusion

The evaluation design is on firm ground, and the windowing result is more interesting than a routine detail. The literature settles the split question but not the overlap question. Because the project measures the overlap effect under leak-free evaluation, it sits in a gap the research notes could not fill from published work, since no study found isolates within-training overlap for high-capacity models. That turns a choice that needed defending into a small contribution, provided the student frames it correctly: more shifted training examples help, measured within recordings, with dataset size not yet separated from overlap.

Two cheap experiments would close the remaining objections. The first is the stride-10 vs stride-150 comparison under protocol B, which answers Dehghani directly. The second is a random split of the stride-150 windows scored against the block split, which would turn "non-overlapping plus random still leaks" from a cited argument into a measured one on this dataset. A third, optional check is to match training-set sizes, for example by subsampling stride-10 windows to the stride-150 count, which would separate the effect of overlap from the effect of having more data.
